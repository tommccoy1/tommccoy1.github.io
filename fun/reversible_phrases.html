Reversible phrases

By Tom McCoy

On this page, I list familiar two-word English phrases that become other familiar two-word phrases when the word order is swapped. Why do I care about such phrases? First, I just think they're fun :) Second, I work in computational linguistics, a field concerned with developing computational models for processing language. Some notable algorithms in this field, referred to as "bag-of-words" approaches, ignore the order of words in a sentence, instead treating a sentence as an unordered collection of words. Such algorithms are motivated by being easy to set up and efficient to run, but they miss the very important fact that word order matters: The meaning of a phrase or sentence is not just determined by which words are in it but also by how those words are put together. Phrases like the ones below are useful reminders of this fact since they highlight how changing word order can substantially change a phrase's interpretation, even among common, familiar phrases (i.e., it's not the case that word order matters only in rare, convoluted examples that require linguistic cleverness to dream up).

Here is the list. I'm playing a bit fast and loose with what counts as a "word" (some of these examples are single compound words rather than two-word phrases):

    1. Chocolate milk: A delicious beverage
    2. Milk chocolate: A delicious treat
    1. Man cave: A guy's private space
    2. Caveman: A person from the Paleolithic
    1. Light green: A pale shade of green
    2. Green light: A signal for a car to go
    1. Animal Farm: A book by George Orwell
    2. Farm animal: An animal that lives on a farm
    1. Cake pan: A pan for baking cakes
    2. Pancake: A particular type of cake made in a pan
    1. Day off: A break from work
    2. Off day: A period when you're not at your best
    1. Another one: An additional item
    2. One another: Each other
    1. First ladies: The wives of US presidents
    2. "Ladies first!": An expression said while holding a door
    1. Back pay: Salary paid after a period when someone was not paid
    2. Payback: Revenge
    1. Dry run: A rehearsal
    2. Run dry: Become completely used up
    1. Blackjack: A card game
    2. Jack Black: A comedic actor
    1. Time travel: A sci-fi plot device
    2. Travel time: How long it takes to get somewhere
    1. "You see?": An expression meaning "What did I tell you?"
    2. "See you!": An expression meaning "Goodbye!"

There are some other examples that I find a bit more boring because the meanings of the two phrases are fairly closely related. However, there's an argument that these phrases are more relevant to the point that word order matters in language because they illustrate the systematic structure of phrases in English rather than depending on idiomatic interpretation. Therefore, here are a few of these examples:

    1. Bird song: Song produced by a bird
    2. Songbird: A bird that sings
    1. Comedy sketch: A humorous skit
    2. Sketch comedy: Genre of humor based on skits
    1. Discussion group: A group whose purpose is discussion
    2. Group discussion: A discussion done by a group
    1. Dish soap: Soap for washing dishes
    2. Soap dish: A dish for holding soap
    1. Guest house: A house for guests
    2. House guest: A guest in one's house
    1. Horse race: A race in which the competitors are horses
    2. Race horse: A horse that does races

There is an xkcd comic based on one such example (houseboat vs. boathouse).
